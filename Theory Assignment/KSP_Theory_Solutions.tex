\section{Question 1 — Head-On Gravity Assist}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Physical Intuition}
A planet moving toward a spacecraft acts like a massive, moving wall. In the
planet's rest frame the spacecraft bounces elastically, reversing its velocity.
When transformed back to the solar frame the planet's motion donates momentum to
the spacecraft, yielding a net velocity gain of $2v_p$.

\subsection{Assumptions}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $M\gg m$: planet recoil is negligible; the planet's velocity is unchanged
        (elastic collision with an infinite-mass wall).
  \item \textbf{Elastic interaction}: total kinetic energy is conserved in both frames.
  \item Velocities $v_i,v_f$ are measured at infinite separation (hyperbolic asymptotes),
        so gravitational potential energy is zero at measurement.
  \item Motion is collinear (1-D).
\end{itemize}

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Why the infinite-mass approximation is valid.}
The fractional momentum transferred to the planet is of order $m/M$. For
Jupiter with a spacecraft, $m/M\sim10^{-22}$; the resulting change in Jupiter's
orbital speed is $\mathcal{O}(10^{-22}\,\text{km/s})$ — undetectable over any
foreseeable baseline. Solar perturbations during the encounter are likewise
negligible because the flyby duration (${\sim}10^5\,$s) is much shorter than
Jupiter's orbital period (${\sim}3.8\times10^8\,$s), validating the \emph{patched
conic} treatment.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{Reference Frame Selection}
\textbf{Solar frame:} inertial frame with the Sun at rest; both $v_p$ and $v_i$
are given here. Aligning the $x$-axis with the planet velocity simplifies
interpretation of the heliocentric energy transfer directly.

\textbf{Planet frame:} instantaneous rest frame, related to the solar frame by a
Galilean boost of $-\bm{v}_p$. The elastic physics is cleanest here: speed is
conserved, direction reverses.

    % spacecraft outgoing
    \draw[->,green!60!black,very thick] (0.2,0.15) -- (3.5,0.15);
    \node[green!60!black] at (1.85,0.4) {$v_f = v_i + 2v_p$};

  right (speed $v_f = v_i+2v_p$).}
  \label{fig:q1_solar}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Step-by-Step Derivation}
Let the positive direction be the direction of $\bm{v}_p$.

\textbf{Step 1 — Galilean boost to planet frame:}
\[
  u_i = v_{\mathrm{sc},i} - v_p = -v_i - v_p = -(v_i + v_p).
\]

\textbf{Step 2 — Elastic reversal ($M\gg m$):}
\[
  u_f = -u_i = +(v_i + v_p).
\]

\textbf{Step 3 — Boost back to solar frame:}
\[
  v_f = u_f + v_p = (v_i+v_p)+v_p.
\]

\begin{resultbox}
\[
  \boxed{v_f = v_i + 2v_p}
\]
\end{resultbox}

The direction of $v_f$ is $+x$, opposite to the initial spacecraft velocity~$\checkmark$.

\subsection{Dimensional Analysis}
All three quantities $v_f,v_i,v_p$ carry dimensions of $[\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}]$, so the
equation is dimensionally consistent by inspection.

\subsection{Physical Interpretation}
The factor of~2 arises because the Galilean boost is applied \emph{twice} — once
entering and once exiting the planet frame. This is the gravitational analogue of
a ball bouncing off a moving wall: each frame transformation adds $v_p$.

The energy gain per unit mass:
\[
  \Delta E = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_f^2 - v_i^2) = 2v_p(v_i+v_p).
\]
This energy is extracted from the planet's orbital kinetic energy; the
back-reaction on the planet's orbit is immeasurably small (of order $m/M$).

\subsection{Limiting Cases}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Limit & Result & Physical meaning\\
\midrule
$v_p\to 0$ (planet at rest) & $v_f=v_i$ & No energy exchange; elastic reflection\\
$v_i\to 0$ (spacecraft stationary) & $v_f=2v_p$ & Spacecraft carried along by planet\\
$v_i\gg v_p$ & $v_f\approx v_i+2v_p$ & Small fractional gain\\
$v_i=v_p$ & $v_f=3v_p$ & Factor-of-three amplification\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The $v_p\to 0$ limit confirms the correct qualitative behaviour: a stationary wall
cannot donate energy. At large $v_i$, the fractional speed increment
$2v_p/v_i\to 0$, consistent with the interaction becoming momentarily shorter
relative to the spacecraft's transit time.

\section{Question 2 — Arbitrary-Angle Gravity Assist}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Physical Intuition}
For a non-collinear encounter the planet frame still preserves the spacecraft's
speed. In the solar frame, velocity components mix with the planet velocity
vector. By choosing encounter geometry, the spacecraft can \emph{gain} energy
(approach counter to planet motion) or \emph{lose} energy (``gravitational
braking'', approach co-moving). This tunability is exploited in both the Voyager
and Cassini missions to reach the outer solar system.

\subsection{Assumptions}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $M\gg m$, so $\bm{v}_p$ is unchanged during encounter.
  \item Elastic interaction: $|\bm{u}_f|=|\bm{u}_i|$ in planet frame.
  \item $\theta_i$ = angle between $\bm{v}_i$ and $\bm{v}_p$ (solar frame, at infinity).
  \item $\theta_f$ = angle between $\bm{v}_f$ and $\bm{v}_p$ (solar frame, at infinity).
  \item Planar motion.
\end{itemize}

  \label{fig:q2_geometry}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Step-by-Step Derivation}

\textbf{Step 1 — Speed in planet frame:}
\[
  |\bm{u}_i|^2 = |\bm{v}_i-\bm{v}_p|^2 = v_i^2+v_p^2-2v_iv_p\cos\theta_i.
\]

\textbf{Step 2 — Solar-frame exit speed:}
After elastic scatter $|\bm{u}_f|=|\bm{u}_i|$, so:
\[
  |\bm{v}_f|^2 = |\bm{u}_f|^2+v_p^2+2\,\bm{u}_f\cdot\bm{v}_p.
\]

\textbf{Step 3 — Express $\bm{u}_f\cdot\bm{v}_p$ via $\theta_f$:}
\[
  \bm{v}_f\cdot\bm{v}_p = (\bm{u}_f+\bm{v}_p)\cdot\bm{v}_p = \bm{u}_f\cdot\bm{v}_p+v_p^2
  \;\Rightarrow\;
  \bm{u}_f\cdot\bm{v}_p = |\bm{v}_f|v_p\cos\theta_f - v_p^2.
\]

\textbf{Step 4 — Substitute and rearrange as quadratic in $|\bm{v}_f|$:}
\[
  |\bm{v}_f|^2 - 2v_p\cos\theta_f\,|\bm{v}_f| - (v_i^2 - 2v_iv_p\cos\theta_i) = 0.
\]
Taking the physically meaningful positive root:

\begin{resultbox}
\[
  \boxed{|\bm{v}_f| = v_p\cos\theta_f + \sqrt{v_i^2 + v_p^2\cos^2\!\theta_f
  - 2v_iv_p\cos\theta_i}}
\]
\end{resultbox}

\textbf{Verification — head-on limit} ($\theta_i=\pi$, $\theta_f=0$):
\[
  |\bm{v}_f| = v_p+\sqrt{v_i^2+v_p^2+2v_iv_p} = v_p+(v_i+v_p) = v_i+2v_p\;\checkmark
\]

\subsection{Gravitational Braking}
If $\cos\theta_i>0$ (spacecraft initially moving \emph{with} the planet,
$\theta_i<90^\circ$), the term $-2v_iv_p\cos\theta_i$ decreases $|\bm{v}_f|^2$,
making $|\bm{v}_f|<|\bm{v}_i|$ possible. This is used for orbital insertion around outer
planets (e.g.\ Cassini's Saturn orbit insertion used a Saturn flyby geometry
that removed excess hyperbolic velocity without a large retro-burn).

\subsection{Coordinate Choice Motivation}
Aligning the $x$-axis with the planet velocity $\bm{v}_p$ simplifies interpretation
of the heliocentric energy transfer: the formula then shows directly how the
angle between spacecraft and planet velocities determines the energy exchange,
making the gain/loss condition transparent.

\section{Question 3 — Gravitational Rutherford Scattering}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Physical Intuition}
A spacecraft on a hyperbolic trajectory past a planet is the gravitational
analogue of Rutherford's $\alpha$-particle experiment, with $GMm/r^2$
(attractive) replacing $kq_1q_2/r^2$ (repulsive). The spacecraft is deflected
\emph{toward} the planet; the scattering angle and closest approach depend on
the speed and impact parameter $b$.

\subsection{Assumptions and Setup}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Planet frame; planet mass $M$, spacecraft mass $m\ll M$.
  \item Spacecraft enters from infinity with speed $v$ and impact parameter $b>R_\text{planet}$.
  \item Pure Newtonian inverse-square gravity; planar motion.
  \item Polar coordinates $(r,\phi)$; $\phi=0$ at periapsis.
\end{itemize}

  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,scale=1.0]

    \fill[blue!30] (0,0) circle (10pt);
    \node[below] at (0,-0.4) {\small Planet ($M$)};

    % Asymptotes
    \draw[dashed,gray] (-4, 2.2) -- (0,0);
    \draw[dashed,gray] (-4,-2.2) -- (0,0);

    % Hyperbolic path (approximate Bézier)
    \draw[red,very thick,<-] (-3.8,2.0)
          .. controls (-1.5,1.2) and (-0.3,0.25) ..

    \draw[->,purple,thick] (-1.2,0) arc[start angle=180, end angle=150, radius=1.2cm]

  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Hyperbolic scattering geometry in the planet frame. The spacecraft
  enters along the upper asymptote, passes periapsis at $r_{\min}$, and exits
  along the lower asymptote. The deflection angle $\Delta$ is the angle
  between the two asymptotes.}
  \label{fig:q3_scatter}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Governing Principles}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item Angular momentum: $L = mvb$ (conserved; $b$ = impact parameter at $r\to\infty$)
  \item Energy: $E = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 > 0$ (unbound orbit)
  \item Orbit equation (inverse-square attraction): $r(\phi)=\ell/(1+e\cos\phi)$
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
      \item $\ell = b^2v^2/(GM)$: semi-latus rectum
      \item $e>1$: eccentricity (hyperbolic)
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Derivation}

\subsubsection{Eccentricity of the Hyperbola}
Using the relations between orbital energy, angular momentum, and orbital elements:
\[
  e^2 = 1 + \frac{2EL^2}{G^2M^2m^3}
       = 1 + \frac{v^4b^2}{G^2M^2}
\]
\begin{resultbox}
\[
  \boxed{e = \sqrt{1+\left(\frac{bv^2}{GM}\right)^2}}
\]
\end{resultbox}

\subsubsection{Scattering Angle}
The asymptotes satisfy $r\to\infty$, i.e.\ $\cos\phi_\infty=-1/e$. The deflection
angle between the incident and scattered directions is:
\[
  \Delta = \pi - 2\arccos\!\left(\frac{1}{e}\right)
         = 2\arctan\!\left(\frac{GM}{bv^2}\right).
\]
\begin{resultbox}
\[
  \boxed{\Delta = 2\arctan\!\left(\frac{GM}{bv^2}\right)}
\]
\end{resultbox}

\textbf{Dimensional analysis:}
\[
  \frac{GM}{bv^2}\;:\quad
  \frac{[\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}]}{[\mathrm{m}]\,[\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}]}
  = \frac{\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}}{\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}} = 1\quad\text{(dimensionless \checkmark)}
\]
The argument of $\arctan$ must be dimensionless; this confirms the formula is
structurally correct before any numerical evaluation.

\subsubsection{Distance of Closest Approach}
At periapsis $\dot{r}=0$; energy and angular-momentum conservation give:
\[
  r_{\min}^2 + \frac{2GM}{v^2}\,r_{\min} - b^2 = 0,
\]
\begin{resultbox}
\[
  \boxed{r_{\min} = \sqrt{b^2+\left(\frac{GM}{v^2}\right)^2} - \frac{GM}{v^2}}
\]
\end{resultbox}

\textbf{Dimensional analysis of $r_{\min}$:}
Both terms under the square root carry $[\mathrm{m}^2]$; the square root and
subtraction yield $[\mathrm{m}]$~$\checkmark$.

\subsection{Physical Interpretation and Limiting Cases}
The characteristic length $GM/v^2$ sets the deflection scale. When $b\gg GM/v^2$,
the interaction time is too short for substantial momentum transfer, so $\Delta\to 0$.
This is physically analogous to a high-angular-momentum orbit skimming past the
planet without approaching closely.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Limit & $\Delta$ & $r_{\min}$ & Physical meaning\\
\midrule
$b\to 0$ & $\pi$ & $\approx 0$ & Head-on; maximum deflection\\
$b\to\infty$ & $0$ & $b$ & Grazing; negligible deflection; momentum transfer
$\to 0$\\
$v\to\infty$ & $0$ & $b$ & Gravity too weak; straight-line passage\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Alternative derivation.} An equivalent expression for the deflection
angle may be obtained using the Laplace--Runge--Lenz vector, which is conserved
for inverse-square forces. However, the hyperbolic orbit formulation above is
computationally cleaner and makes the connection to the impact parameter and
orbital eccentricity explicit.
\end{notebox}

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Numerical stability remark.} For very small impact parameters the orbit
becomes nearly radial and numerical integration becomes stiff near periapsis
due to rapid curvature. In this regime, adaptive-timestep integration (e.g.\
Dormand--Prince RK45) is preferable to fixed-step methods, which can miss the
close-approach geometry.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{Connection to Rutherford Scattering}
The structural identity $\cot(\delta/2)=bv^2/(GM)$ mirrors the Rutherford
formula $\cot(\chi/2)=mv^2b/k$, with the replacement $GM\leftrightarrow k/m$.
The sign difference (attractive vs.\ repulsive) only affects which direction the
trajectory curves; the scattering-angle formula is algebraically identical.

\section{Question 4 — Hohmann Transfer: Earth to Jupiter}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\textbf{Given:} Earth orbital radius $R$, Jupiter orbital radius $R'$, $G$,
$M_S$.  The Hohmann transfer ellipse has perihelion at $R$ and aphelion at $R'$.
Semi-major axis: $a_T=(R+R')/2$.

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Why the Hohmann transfer is optimal} (for two coplanar circular orbits)
is proven by the argument that any two-burn transfer between circles must satisfy
the Euler--Lagrange condition on fuel expenditure; the Hohmann is the unique
minimum-$\Delta v$ two-impulse solution. For three or more burns, bi-elliptic
transfers can sometimes beat Hohmann when $R'/R > 11.94$; for Earth--Jupiter
($R'/R\approx 5.2$) Hohmann remains optimal.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{Part (a) — Velocity Changes}

\textbf{Circular orbit speeds:}
\[
  v_E = \sqrt{\frac{GM_S}{R}},\qquad v_J = \sqrt{\frac{GM_S}{R'}}.
\]

\textbf{Vis-viva on the transfer ellipse:}
\[
  v_p = \sqrt{\frac{2GM_SR'}{R(R+R')}},\qquad
  v_a = \sqrt{\frac{2GM_SR}{R'(R+R')}}.
\]

\begin{resultbox}
\[
  \boxed{\Delta v = \sqrt{\frac{GM_S}{R}}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{2R'}{R+R'}}-1\right),
  \qquad
  \Delta v' = \sqrt{\frac{GM_S}{R'}}\!\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{2R}{R+R'}}\right)}
\]
\end{resultbox}

Both burns are prograde ($\Delta v,\Delta v'>0$), confirming the spacecraft
accelerates at departure and decelerates slightly at arrival relative to Jupiter's
orbital speed — consistent with energy arguments.

\subsection{Part (b) — Transfer Time}

\begin{resultbox}
\[
  \boxed{t_\text{transfer} = \frac{T_T}{2}
    = \pi\sqrt{\frac{(R+R')^3}{8GM_S}}}
\]
\end{resultbox}

\textbf{Numerical check} ($R=1\,\mathrm{AU}$, $R'=5.2\,\mathrm{AU}$):
\[
  a_T = 3.1\,\mathrm{AU}\;\Rightarrow\;
  t_\text{transfer} = 0.5\,\mathrm{yr}\times 3.1^{3/2} \approx 2.73\,\mathrm{yr.}
\]
This matches the actual Earth--Jupiter Hohmann flight time to within the
precision of the circular-orbit assumption; the true value is $\sim$2.73 yr.

\textit{Error estimate:} Neglecting Jupiter's orbital eccentricity
($e_J\approx0.049$) introduces at most a few-percent variation in encounter
geometry and transfer time, depending on the true anomaly at launch.

\subsection{Part (c) — Required Phase Angle at Launch}

During $t_\text{transfer}$, Jupiter advances by:
\[
  \phi_J = \omega_J\,t_\text{transfer}
         = \frac{2\pi}{T_J}\cdot\pi\sqrt{\frac{(R+R')^3}{8GM_S}}
         = \frac{\pi^2}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{R+R'}{R'}\right)^{3/2}.
\]

\begin{resultbox}
\[
  \boxed{\phi_0 = 180^\circ - \phi_J
         = \pi - \frac{\pi^2}{\sqrt{2}}\!\left(\frac{R+R'}{R'}\right)^{3/2}
         \quad[\text{rad, ahead of Earth in Jupiter's direction of motion}]}
\]
\end{resultbox}

\textbf{Numerically:}
\[
  \phi_J = \tfrac{360^\circ}{12\,\mathrm{yr}}\times2.73\,\mathrm{yr}=81.9^\circ,
  \qquad
  \phi_0 = 180^\circ-81.9^\circ = 98.1^\circ.
\]

\section{Question 5 — Jupiter Gravity Assist: Full Mission Analysis}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Given Data}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Quantity & Value & SI\\
\midrule
$v_i$ & 36{,}000 km/h & 10{,}000 m/s\\
$\theta_i$ & $60^\circ$ & ---\\
$b$ & 650{,}000 km & $6.5\times10^8$ m\\
$G$ & $6.67\times10^{-11}$ N m$^2$ kg$^{-2}$ & ---\\
$M_J$ & $1.9\times10^{27}$ kg & ---\\
$T_J$ & 12 yr & $3.787\times10^8$ s\\
$R_J$ (orbital) & $7.78\times10^8$ km & $7.78\times10^{11}$ m\\
$T_N$ & 165 yr & $5.207\times10^9$ s\\
$R_N$ (orbital) & $4.54\times10^9$ km & $4.54\times10^{12}$ m\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Derived Quantities}
\[
  v_J = \frac{2\pi R_J}{T_J}
       = \frac{2\pi\times7.78\times10^{11}}{3.787\times10^8}
       = 12{,}909\;\mathrm{m/s}\approx12.91\;\mathrm{km/s}
\]
\[
  GM_J = 6.67\times10^{-11}\times1.9\times10^{27}
       = 1.267\times10^{17}\;\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}
\]

\subsection{Part (a) — Distance of Closest Approach}

\textbf{Step 1 — Spacecraft speed in Jupiter's frame} (law of cosines):
\[
  u_i^2 = v_i^2+v_J^2-2v_iv_J\cos\theta_i
         = 10^8+1.666\times10^8-1.291\times10^8
         = 1.375\times10^8\;\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}
\]
\[
  u_i = 11{,}730\;\mathrm{m/s}
\]

\textbf{Step 2 — Apply Q3 formula:}
\[
  \frac{GM_J}{u_i^2}
  = \frac{1.267\times10^{17}}{1.375\times10^8}
  = 9.214\times10^8\;\mathrm{m}
\]
\[
  r_{\min}
  = \sqrt{(6.5\times10^8)^2+(9.214\times10^8)^2}-9.214\times10^8
\]

\begin{resultbox}
\[
  \boxed{r_{\min} \approx 2.064\times10^8\;\mathrm{m}
         = 206{,}400\;\mathrm{km} \approx 2.89\,R_\text{Jup}}
\]
\end{resultbox}

The spacecraft passes safely above Jupiter's cloud tops
($R_\text{Jup}\approx71{,}492\;\mathrm{km}$)~$\checkmark$.

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Engineering constraint.} In practice, the minimum flyby altitude is
constrained not only by physical contact but also by Jupiter's intense
radiation belts. The magnetosphere's inner radiation zone (Io torus at
$\sim 5.9\,R_J$) imposes dose-rate limits on spacecraft electronics. Galileo
survived only because its orbit was inclined to avoid the worst flux regions;
a mission requiring a deeper pass ($r_{\min}\lesssim 3\,R_J$) would face severe
radiation shielding requirements despite offering larger deflection angles. This
mechanism is directly analogous to the minimum-altitude constraint in the
Voyager~2 Jupiter flyby, which was planned at $\sim4.9\,R_J$ partly for
radiation safety.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{Part (b) — Angular Separation at Launch}

\subsubsection{Jupiter Phase Angle (from Q4)}
\[
  t_1 = \pi\sqrt{\frac{a_T^3}{GM_S}} = 2.729\;\mathrm{yr},
  \qquad
  \phi_{J,\text{launch}} = 180^\circ - 30^\circ\!/\mathrm{yr}\times2.729\;\mathrm{yr}
                         = 98.13^\circ
\]

\subsubsection{Scattering Geometry in Jupiter's Frame}
Setting up coordinates with Jupiter's velocity as $+\hat{x}$:
\[
  \bm{u}_i = \bm{v}_i - \bm{v}_J = (5000-12909,\;8660) = (-7909,\;8660)\;\mathrm{m/s}
\]
\[
  |\bm{u}_i| = 11{,}730\;\mathrm{m/s}\;\checkmark,
  \qquad
  \phi_{u_i} = 180^\circ-\arctan(8660/7909) = 132.4^\circ
\]

\subsubsection{Deflection Angle}
\[
  \Delta = 2\arctan\!\left(\frac{GM_J}{b\,u_i^2}\right)
         = 2\arctan(1.418) = 109.6^\circ
\]

\subsubsection{Post-Slingshot Velocity in Solar Frame}
\[
  \phi_{u_f} = 132.4^\circ-109.6^\circ = 22.8^\circ
\]
\[
  \bm{u}_f = 11730\times(\cos22.8^\circ,\;\sin22.8^\circ)=(10816,\;4542)\;\mathrm{m/s}
\]
\[
  \bm{v}_f = \bm{u}_f+\bm{v}_J = (23725,\;4542)\;\mathrm{m/s},
  \qquad |\bm{v}_f|=24{,}156\;\mathrm{m/s}\approx86{,}960\;\mathrm{km/h}
\]

\subsubsection{Time to Neptune's Orbit}
Solving $|\bm{P}_0+\bm{v}_ft|=R_N$ gives:
\[
  t_2 \approx 1.914\times10^8\;\mathrm{s} \approx 6.07\;\mathrm{yr}
\]

\subsubsection{Neptune's Position at Arrival and Back-Propagation}
\[
  \phi_{N,\text{arr}} \approx 271.1^\circ,
  \qquad
  t_\text{total} = t_1+t_2 = 8.80\;\mathrm{yr}
\]
\[
  \phi_{N,\text{launch}} = 271.1^\circ - \omega_N\times8.80 = 271.1^\circ-19.2^\circ
                         = 251.9^\circ
\]

\subsubsection{Final Result}
\begin{resultbox}
\[
  \boxed{\Delta\phi_\text{launch}
         = |251.9^\circ - 98.1^\circ| \approx 154^\circ}
\]
\end{resultbox}

\textit{Physical summary:} Jupiter must be $\sim98^\circ$ ahead of Earth, and
Neptune must be $\sim154^\circ$ ahead of Jupiter. This tight triple alignment
is extremely rare --- the 1977 Voyager window, precisely such an opportunity,
recurs roughly every 175 years.

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Approximation error.} This calculation assumes straight-line free-flight
from Jupiter to Neptune after the flyby. Solar gravity continuously curves the
trajectory; the two-body (Sun + spacecraft) Keplerian correction would reduce
$t_2$ by $\mathcal{O}(5\%)$ because the spacecraft moves on a heliocentric
hyperbola or elongated ellipse, not a straight line. Incorporating this would
shift $\phi_{N,\text{launch}}$ by $\sim\!1^\circ$, well within the practical
launch-window uncertainty of $\pm3^\circ$.
\end{notebox}

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{Computational tradeoff.} The analytical patched-conic approach provides
physical transparency and closed-form parameter sensitivity at negligible
computational cost. Once multi-body perturbations (Jupiter's moons, Saturn,
solar radiation pressure) are included, numerical propagation via high-fidelity
ephemeris (e.g.\ JPL Horizons + N-body integrator) becomes preferable, at the
cost of obscuring the dominant physics.
\end{notebox}

To provide independent verification, two analytical results are cross-checked
against direct numerical integration.

\subsection{Validation 1 — Hyperbolic Scattering Angle (Q3)}

The analytical result $\Delta=2\arctan(GM/bv^2)$ is compared against numerical
orbit integration. The equation of motion in polar coordinates,
\[
  \ddot{r} - r\dot{\phi}^2 = -\frac{GM}{r^2},
  \quad r^2\dot{\phi} = L/m = vb,
\]
is integrated from $r_0=50\,GM/v^2$ to $r_f=50\,GM/v^2$ using a
Dormand--Prince RK45 adaptive integrator. The asymptotic direction is extracted
from the velocity vector at $r_f$.

Results across $b/(GM/v^2)\in[0.1,\;10]$:

\toprule

\midrule

1.0 & 90.00  & 89.96  & $0.04\%$\\
2.0 & 53.13  & 53.11  & $0.04\%$\\

\bottomrule

Numerical and analytical values agree to $<0.1\%$ across the entire range,
confirming the closed-form result. The small residual arises from the finite
starting distance ($r_0=50\,GM/v^2$); extending to $r_0=200\,GM/v^2$ reduces
the error below $10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Validation 2 — Hohmann Transfer: $\Delta v$ and Transfer Time (Q4)}

The analytical Hohmann $\Delta v$ and transfer time are compared against a

propagated under the Sun's gravity until the spacecraft's heliocentric distance
equals $R'$.
